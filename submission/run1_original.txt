│  effect on Jan 1 2022 requires most U.S. hospitals to post a          │
│  machine‑readable file that lists three numbers for each procedure:   │
│  the original “gross” charge, the amount negotiated with insurers,    │
│  and the cash price a patient would pay without insurance. The goal   │
│  is to let patients compare costs before they choose care.            │

│  55 % of hospitals fully obeyed the rule, and many of the published   │

│  Because the data are technical—filled with CPT codes and bundled     │
│  charges—fewer than 5 % of shoppers actually use them, according to   │
│  a 2022 RAND study. Patients often lack the clinical knowledge to     │
│  interpret the numbers, and the websites are not user‑friendly.       │
│                                                                       │
│  State governments are stepping in. By 2024, twelve states,           │
│  including California, Colorado, and Washington, have passed          │
│  stricter laws that demand real‑time price estimates for scheduled    │
│  procedures and impose penalties of $10,000 or more per violation.    │
│                                                                       │
│  Researchers estimate that full, accurate transparency could cut      │
│  national health‑care spending by 2–4 %—roughly $30–$60 billion a     │
│  year—by encouraging price competition and smarter site selection.    │
│  The big challenges remain: turning raw price files into tools        │

│  compliance to Medicare payments—that push hospitals toward           │

│  empower consumers and drive a more affordable health system.         │
