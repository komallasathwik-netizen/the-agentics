=== FINAL OUTPUT (also saved to output/explainer.md) ===


Imagine you walk into a grocery store and see a sign: “Apples $2 per lb, Bananas $1 per lb.” You know exactly what you’ll pay. Price‑transparency rules try to give you that same clarity for medical care.  

In January 2022 the federal government required most hospitals to post a “shoppable services” list with at least 300 common procedures—MRI, colonoscopy, blood test—showing three numbers: the sticker price (gross charge), the cash price you might pay without insurance, and the amount your insurer has negotiated [source needed].  

The idea is to let you compare hospitals the way you compare grocery prices. Early research found that when patients saw a simple side‑by‑side comparison, roughly one‑quarter chose a lower‑cost provider and saved about $1,200 per case [source needed].  

The reality is messier. Only about 55 % of hospitals fully comply, and many lists are buried on the website or written in confusing codes [source needed]. Even when you find a price, the same MRI can range from $300 to $1,500—a 400 % spread—because each hospital negotiates its own contracts and faces different local costs [source needed].  

More than 20 states have added their own price‑calculator rules, sometimes conflicting with the federal rule [source needed].  

**Practical steps**  
1. Visit the hospital’s website and look for “Price Transparency,” “Shoppable Services,” or a similar link—often under Billing or Patient Resources.  
2. Download the price list and locate your procedure.  
3. Compare the cash price with nearby hospitals using free tools such as Fair Health or Healthcare Bluebook [source needed].  
4. Call the billing office to confirm the cash price and ask about any discounts before you schedule the appointment.  
5. Write down the numbers and keep them for reference.
